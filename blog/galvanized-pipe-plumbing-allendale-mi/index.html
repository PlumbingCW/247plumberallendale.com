Galvanized Pipe in Allendale, MI Homes: What to Know

Most Allendale homes are too new to have galvanized steel pipe inside the walls. If yours was built before about 1980, or you are buying an older house or farmhouse, it is worth a look, and this guide shows how.

It covers how to recognize the pipe, what the common symptoms mean, and how to decide between patching and repiping.

Short answer: a minority issue here, but not a zero issue

Galvanized steel supply pipe went into older houses and mostly stopped going into new ones long ago. It rusts from the inside, narrows over time and eventually leaks or starves fixtures of pressure. If your home is recent, you very likely have copper or plastic instead, and this problem is not yours.

The exceptions are older houses, farmhouses and homes where an earlier owner never updated the pipes. Those are the ones worth checking. You do not need tools to start.

Allendale, MI: a young housing stock with an older minority

Census data (American Community Survey 2020 to 2024) puts the median year built for Allendale Township homes at 2003. Of 8,454 housing units, about 1,368 (around 16 percent) were built before 1980 and 298 (3.5 percent) before 1940. Check your own build year on your closing papers or county property record, and if it falls before 1980, look at the pipes in your basement or utility room.

The township's 2025 water quality report counts 3,979 service lines and says zero are known or presumed lead. That covers the line from the main to your meter, not the pipes inside your walls. Older towns nearby do skew older: the same survey gives a median year built of 1973 for the Jenison area and 1984 for Walker, where about 45 percent of homes predate 1980.

Patch it or repipe it?

Repairing a single leak in galvanized pipe is often possible. The question is what is likely to fail next.

Spot repair and monitoring

  • Fits when a short section has leaked and the rest is sound.
  • Joins old pipe to new, and old threads can fail next to the repair.
  • Leaves reduced flow in the remaining pipe.
  • Makes sense for a few more years in an otherwise stable house.

Repiping

  • Replaces the aging runs with new pipe.
  • Fits repeated leaks, bad pressure or discolored water.
  • Often easier to plan before a leak damages a finished wall.
  • Worth pricing before a sale, a remodel or a second leak.

How to check your own pipes in five minutes

Go to the basement or utility room and find the pipe that runs from the meter to the water heater. Galvanized steel is dull gray, has threaded fittings and attracts a magnet. Copper is brown or green and does not, and plastic is white, blue or red.

Scratching a small spot with a coin on gray pipe shows silvery metal if it is galvanized. Take a picture and note how many runs look like it. That alone makes the conversation with a plumber shorter and clearer.

Do not assume that an old galvanized system is a lead problem or that a new one is safe. The water report notes that homes with a lead or galvanized service line requiring replacement may need to flush for at least 5 minutes. Ask the township utility what is on your side of the meter.

Planning a repipe and finding the leaks first

Repiping is normally planned around the house: which walls open, which fixtures are affected, and how water gets turned off during the work. A repiping estimate should list the runs that will be replaced and say what is left. Ask for the clear price before work starts.

If you are not sure whether a pressure problem or a stain comes from the pipe or from something else, leak detection can find where water is going without opening walls at random. That step often decides whether a repair is enough.

The older end looks different. A nearby city's utility runs a program to replace galvanized service lines, and the owner stays responsible for the pipes past the first valve inside the building. If you live in Walker or Jenison, ask your own utility where its responsibility ends.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if my pipes are galvanized?

Look for dull gray metal pipe with threaded fittings, often with rust at the joints. A magnet sticks to it, but not to copper or plastic. If you are unsure, send us a description and we will tell you what to confirm.

Is galvanized pipe dangerous?

It is mainly a durability and flow problem as it corrodes inside. Galvanized pipe that was once connected to lead is a separate concern, so ask your water utility about the service line and test your tap if you are worried.

Why is the water pressure low in just one bathroom?

Buildup can narrow one run while the rest stay normal, but low pressure also comes from a clogged aerator, a partly closed valve or a leak. Compare fixtures and call us with the pattern.

Can I repair one section of galvanized pipe?

Often, yes. The risk is that the old pipe next to the repair is just as thin, so another leak can follow. Ask what condition the rest of the run is in before you decide.

Does homeowners insurance pay for repiping?

Usually only for sudden damage such as a burst pipe. Gradual corrosion and routine replacement are typically excluded. Check your own policy before you assume either way.

Will repiping mean opening a lot of walls?

It depends on the layout. Basement runs are often exposed already, while pipes inside finished walls take more planning. A plumber should describe which walls open before work starts.

Is galvanized pipe common in Allendale homes?

Not as a general rule. The median home here was built in 2003 and only about 16 percent predate 1980. Older farmhouses and early rentals are the likely exceptions, and nearby towns like Jenison and Walker skew older.
