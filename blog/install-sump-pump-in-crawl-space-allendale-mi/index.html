How to Install a Sump Pump in a Crawl Space (Allendale Homes)

Short answer: cut a pit at the lowest point of the crawl space and set a perforated liner in gravel. Stand the pump on a solid base inside it, add a check valve on the discharge line, and run that line outdoors to ground that slopes away from the house. Plug the pump into a GFCI-protected outlet.

The pipe work is the easy half. Whether you need a pump at all, where the water goes and how the pump gets power are the choices that decide whether it works. This page walks through them, and tells you where a licensed electrician or a plumber should take over.

Signs a crawl space may need a pump

Not every damp crawl space needs one. Gutters and grading come first, and we cover that below.

Water after thaw or hard rain.

Standing water or a wet ground cover in a low spot after the spring thaw or a long rain is the usual reason for a pit.

Musty smell and wet insulation.

A damp smell in the house, or floor insulation that is wet or sagging, can point to moisture underneath.

Moisture after the gutters are fixed.

If the roof water is handled and the crawl space still floods, a pit gives the water somewhere to go.

Fix the cheap sources of water before you cut a pit

Roof water is a common culprit. Make sure gutters are clear, downspouts end several feet from the foundation, and the soil slopes away from the house.

If the crawl space is still wet, a leaking supply or drain pipe is worth ruling out. A pump will keep removing water that a broken pipe keeps adding. Our sump pump work begins with that question, and if water seeps through walls or floor, basement waterproofing is the next conversation.

How the pit and pump go in

Pick the low spot and cut the pit.

Choose the lowest part of the crawl space, ideally close to where the discharge will leave the house. Dig deep enough that the liner rim sits at the ground surface, and lay a few inches of gravel in the bottom.

Set the liner.

Place the perforated liner, backfill around it with gravel, and make sure it is level. A lid keeps debris out and crawl space air from drifting up through the pit.

Install pump, pipe and check valve.

Stand the pump on a flat base so it does not pull in silt. Add the check valve a short way above the pump, so water in the pipe does not run back into the pit. Make sure the float swings freely and does not touch the wall.

Run the discharge and test.

Take the line out through the rim joist or foundation wall with a sealed opening. Pour in a bucket of water, watch the float rise and the pump start, and confirm water leaves the outlet.

Where the discharge goes, and where it must not

Send the water outside, well away from the foundation, onto ground that slopes away from your house and your neighbor's. Avoid pointing it at a driveway where it will ice over, and keep it off a neighbor's lot. Slope any outdoor run downhill so it drains, because a full pipe can freeze in winter.

The sanitary sewer is not where a sump pump belongs, and clean groundwater does not belong in that system. Whether any other tie-in is allowed depends on where you live, so ask your township or inspector what is allowed before you plan the route. We are not going to quote a rule here, because the answer is theirs to give.

Call MISS DIG 811 before the discharge trench

Call 811 or go to missdig811.org at least 3 business days before any digging, for a project of any size. It costs nothing and the line is open around the clock. Public utilities get marked, but private lines such as a yard light feed or a lawn irrigation line are not, so look for those yourself before the shovel goes in.

Power: GFCI, a dedicated outlet and a backup

Plug the pump into a GFCI-protected outlet, ideally one that serves only the pump. Do not run an extension cord as a permanent fix. If there is no outlet near the pit, adding one is a job for a licensed electrician, and we will tell you plainly when a job needs one.

A battery backup or a second pump helps when a storm knocks out power. Test the float and pump with a bucket of water in spring and again before winter.

Allendale, MI: river land and the flood map

The Grand River forms the northern and eastern boundaries of Allendale Township, and the township's Master Plan notes that much of the land next to it is in the FEMA 100-year floodplain. Bass River State Recreation Area (1,665 acres) and the Bass River basin wetlands sit in the northwest. If your home is near either, look up your parcel on the FEMA flood map and ask the county or township what that means before you cut a pit.

A flood map is a planning tool, not a report on your crawl space. A parcel outside the mapped floodplain can still have a wet crawl space, and one inside it can stay dry. Watch your own crawl space after the next hard rain or thaw, and write down what you see.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should a sump pit be in a crawl space?

Deep enough that the liner rim sits at the ground surface and the float has room to rise and fall. The exact depth depends on your liner and pump, so follow their instructions. A pit that is too shallow short-cycles the pump.

Do I really need a check valve?

Yes. Without one, the water in the discharge pipe runs back into the pit every time the pump stops, and the pump restarts constantly. Put the valve a short way above the pump and check the arrow points out.

Will the discharge line freeze in winter?

It can if the line holds water. Slope outdoor runs downhill so they empty after each cycle, and keep the outlet clear of ice and snow. If a pipe has frozen before, call us about rerouting it.

Do I need a permit to install a sump pump?

We cannot say for your address. Ask the township or its inspector what applies to the pump, the electrical work and the discharge.

Should I add a battery backup?

It is a good idea where the pump matters, because storms are when power fails and water rises. Run a bucket test on both pumps at least twice a year.

Does living near the Grand River mean I need a sump pump in Allendale?

Not automatically. Check the FEMA flood map for your parcel, ask the county or township what applies, and look at your crawl space after a hard rain. If it holds water, call us and we will talk it through.
